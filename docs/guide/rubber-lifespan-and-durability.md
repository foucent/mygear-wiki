# How Long Do Table Tennis Rubbers Last?

Which rubber lasts longest is the question every player asks sooner or later. Mainstream sheets fall into three families — **Chinese**, **Japanese** and **German** — and they do not age at the same rate, or in the same way.

---

## Three Families, One Ranking

The three families are the **Chinese** tacky sheets, the **Japanese** sheets, and the **German** sheets. Ranked purely on durability, the order is:

1. **Chinese** — the most durable by a clear margin
2. **Japanese** — second, and much better than German
3. **German** — no durable option among the mainstream brands

That ranking surprises people, because the loudest complaint about Chinese rubbers is that they don't last. So it's worth separating what actually goes wrong.

---

## Chinese Rubbers: Hurricane and Oxidation

The main criticism of **Hurricane** is that it oxidises. It does — it is the most oxidation-prone sheet of the three. The topsheet reacts with air, and after enough exposure it looks dull and slightly swollen.

But that oxidation is mostly cosmetic. It **does not meaningfully affect how the rubber plays**.

Set oxidation aside and look at the two things that genuinely decide a rubber's life:

- **Topsheet wear resistance** — how hard it is to wear through
- **Sponge resilience** — how well the sponge holds up to repeated impact

On both counts, **DHS** sheets are the toughest of the three families.

---

## Japanese Rubbers: Butterfly

Among Japanese sheets, **Butterfly** is the reference. On the two hard measures — sponge life and topsheet wear resistance — it is **not** as durable as Hurricane.

Its advantage is the one thing Hurricane cannot offer: **it does not oxidise**. Leave it however you like, treat it however you like, and it keeps playing. You never get the depressing sight of a sheet that has gone dull and swollen because it wasn't stored properly after boosting.

So the trade is straightforward: a shorter natural life than a Chinese sheet, but no storage anxiety — and a **much** better life than anything German.

---

## German Rubbers: Crisp, Fast, Short-Lived

Of all the German sheets, the reviewer found **not one** durable option — not from **Tibhar**, **Donic** or **XIOM**, the mainstream names. The family's real weakness is the topsheet: it pills and scales up easily, and not only under heavy use. Put a little power through the ball, or brush it a bit more than usual, and the surface starts going.

The most striking example: a flagship Tibhar sheet, tested fresh on release. One hour of play was enough to raise light pilling on the topsheet. An hour.

The sponge doesn't do much better over the long run. It is relatively short-lived next to Chinese and Japanese sheets. What you get instead is the German signature — a crisp, fast sheet that receives and redirects well, with the metallic crack players either love or can't stand. What you lose is the top end: play it long enough and the sponge softens, the performance visibly drops off, and it stops holding up your shots.

## Durability at a Glance

| | **Chinese** (Hurricane) | **Japanese** (Butterfly) | **German** (Tibhar / Donic / XIOM) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Topsheet wear | Best — hardest to wear out | Below Hurricane | Worst — pills easily |
| Sponge life | Toughest, most resilient | Below Hurricane | Softens and drops off |
| Oxidation | The one real weakness | Does not oxidise | — |
| Overall durability | **1st** | **2nd** | **3rd** |
